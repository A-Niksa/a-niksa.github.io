Circular Sorting: Three Improved Bounds with Lean-Checked Certificates

Arsha Niksa · 25 October 2026 · AI-assisted mathematical exploration

I first encountered the prime-number case of circular sorting as a problem in the National Mathematics Olympiad organized by the Iranian Mathematical Society. It is the sort of question that looks like a puzzle about swapping numbers and then quietly turns into a problem about permutation groups.

After thinking about that case, I became curious about the general problem: how difficult can circular sorting become when the number of labels is composite? I asked Astra, an AI model, to investigate the existing bounds, try to improve them, and look for something genuinely new. It found three explicit examples that improve lower bounds reported in a recent paper. The examples come with finite certificates checked by Lean.

Authorship disclosure. I initiated this exploration and posed the challenge to Astra. The mathematical explanations in this post are AI-written, as are the computational constructions and formal certificate code. I am not a combinatorics expert by any means. I am sharing the result, the precise evidence for it, and its limitations openly so that others can inspect or improve it.

1. What is circular sorting?

Place the numbers \(0,1,\ldots,n-1\) around a circle, one at each position. A move swaps any two labels, not necessarily adjacent ones. The goal is to put the labels in cyclic order. Rotating the entire circle does not change the configuration, but reversing it generally does.

For a particular circular configuration, count the fewest swaps needed to sort it. Then maximize that number over all possible configurations. Call the resulting worst-case distance \(x(n)\). The prime case is especially elegant: for a prime \(p\), one has \(x(p)=p-2\). For composite \(n\), the picture is much less complete [1][2].

2. Three improved lower bounds

The August 2026 version of a paper by Bastide, Bishnoi, Groenland, Gijswijt and Joshi gives the following intervals in its Table 1 [2]. The explicit circular permutations supplied here raise the lower endpoint by one in three cases:

Number of labelsBounds in [2]Bounds from these certificates
\(n=36\)\(31\le x(36)\le33\)\(32\le x(36)\le33\)
\(n=40\)\(35\le x(40)\le37\)\(36\le x(40)\le37\)
\(n=42\)\(37\le x(42)\le39\)\(38\le x(42)\le39\)

These are lower-bound improvements. They do not give the exact diameter at any of these three sizes, nor do they resolve the general composite case. The upper bounds are known; here the evidence for the new lower bounds consists of concrete witnesses.

3. Why a sorting problem becomes a cycle-count problem

Write a permutation \(p\) in one-line notation: the entry \(p(i)\) gives the label at position \(i\). Let \(r(i)=i+1\pmod n\) denote a rotation, and let \(C(q)\) be the number of disjoint cycles of the permutation \(q\), including fixed points.

The key elementary fact is that a permutation \(q\) requires exactly \(n-C(q)\) arbitrary transpositions to turn it into the identity. One swap can change the number of cycles by only one, while a cycle of length \(\ell\) can be broken into singletons with \(\ell-1\) swaps. Summing over disjoint cycles proves the formula.

The entire witness question is therefore reduced to finding a permutation whose every rotation has few cycles. In these three examples, no rotation has more than four cycles. That makes each witness exactly \(n-4\) swaps away from the sorted circle.

4. A witness that needs 32 swaps

For \(n=36\), Astra found the following one-line permutation, indexed from zero:

p = [17,32,8,24,5,20,14,15,13,19,29,2,
     25,10,12,6,22,7,30,11,16,23,1,28,
     31,3,21,0,27,33,26,4,35,18,9,34]

For this permutation, \(C(p)=1\). Among the other rotations, the cycle count is two at shifts \(7\) and \(23\), three at each nonzero even shift, and four at the remaining odd shifts. Thus

\[ \max_{0\le k<36}C(r^kp)=4,\qquad d([p],[\mathrm{id}])=36-4=32. \]

A single example is enough to prove \(x(36)\ge32\), because \(x(36)\) is a maximum. The same idea works at \(n=40\) and \(n=42\); their complete permutations and all cycle decompositions are published below rather than taking up several pages here.

5. What exactly has Lean checked?

The finite certificates were checked using Lean 4.19.0. The source uses decide +kernel and contains no sorry, added axioms, native_decide, or outside proof oracle. The supplied Lean check log reports that the fifteen printed theorem checks depend on no axioms.

For each of the three sizes, the Lean file checks:

  1. The witness has exactly \(n\) entries and uses each label once.
  2. For every shift \(k\), the listed nonempty cycles cover the entire set of labels, with no repetitions, and each arrow is correct for the rotated permutation.
  3. The full vector of cycle counts agrees with the claimed profile.
  4. Some shift actually has four cycles.
  5. The minimum certified swap cost is \(32\), \(36\), or \(38\), respectively.

This is a significant but limited formal guarantee. Lean certifies the explicit permutations and their cycle data. The general theorem that transposition length equals \(n-C(q)\), the interpretation of circular distance, and the general upper-bound argument are supplied as ordinary mathematical reasoning in the accompanying README. They are not formalized end-to-end in Lean. The certificates also do not establish mathematical novelty by themselves.

6. How the examples were found and independently checked

Astra used a stochastic search in C++ to look for permutations that minimize the number of rotations with more than four cycles. Simulated annealing proposed swaps and three-cycle mutations; the search histories are available with the code. Stochastic search is a discovery method, not a proof: running it again need not produce the same permutation.

What makes these examples reproducible is that the results do not require rerunning the search. The finite witnesses are saved explicitly, and a separate Python verifier reconstructs all cycles from the permutations. That independent traversal confirms the claimed distances for all \(36+40+42=118\) rotations. It uses only the Python standard library.

The general upper bound \(x(n)\le n-3\) for even \(n\ge4\) follows from a parity argument already discussed in [2]: consecutive rotations have opposite permutation parity when \(n\) is even, ruling out the special cycle structure required to attain distance \(n-2\). Combining this known upper bound with the explicit lower-bound examples gives the intervals above.

7. Download the certificates

All files are plain text and can be inspected directly. To repeat the formal checks, install Lean 4.19.0, then run lean CircularSorting.lean; run python3 verify.py for the independent cycle traversal.

  • CircularSorting.lean — Lean theorems and all explicit cycle certificates.
  • witnesses.json — permutations, cycle counts and decompositions.
  • verify.py — independent standard-library Python checker.
  • README.md — complete mathematical proof, novelty scope, and reproducibility notes.
  • Lean check log and Python check log — outputs supplied with the certificates.
  • search.cpp — heuristic search code, with 36, 40 and 42 search logs.
  • Browse the complete certificate directory on GitHub.

8. How new is the result?

The precise claim is an improvement over the publicly available bounds in Table 1 of [2]. That is a narrower and more honest statement than claiming to have solved the circular-sorting problem or proved that these examples are the first ever found. An independent discovery, a later improvement, or related results elsewhere may exist. The values of \(x(36)\), \(x(40)\), and \(x(42)\) are still not pinned down exactly by this work.

For me, the interesting part is also methodological. I started with an Olympiad question whose prime case intrigued me; a request to Astra led to larger computational witnesses, and formal certificates made those witnesses checkable without trusting the AI or its search procedure. I do not pretend this makes me a specialist in combinatorics. It does make the claim concrete enough for specialists to scrutinize.
